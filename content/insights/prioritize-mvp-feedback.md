Your MVP is live. People are trying it. Now the suggestions are arriving: add a dashboard, simplify onboarding, change the buttons, build a mobile app.

Which feedback should become your next development task?

Start with the user’s goal. Observe where they struggle, separate the problem from their proposed solution, and prioritize the issue that most interferes with the product’s core value. Then write a small, testable development brief and check whether the change helps users complete the task.

That process gives a founder something more useful than a growing feature list: a reason for what to build next.

## A lesson from The Builder's Table

During the October 1, 2026 episode of The Builder's Table, participants tried Read Me, a conversation-card game, and discussed the experience with its builder, Amadeu Tolentino.

The discussion returned to a useful principle: put the product in people’s hands, learn from the interaction, and shorten the time between feedback and the next iteration. Darrel Frater emphasized the value of hearing reactions while someone is using a product. Touré Young emphasized letting people experience it without steering their conclusions.

Amadeu also described his intention to incorporate feedback into the game quickly. That was a plan for the next iteration, rather than a report of completed improvements.

The six steps below translate those themes into a practical process for founders. They are an editorial framework inspired by the session, rather than a transcript of a framework presented on the show.

## 1. Define the task before collecting opinions

An MVP should help a particular user accomplish something specific. Write that outcome in a sentence before your next feedback session.

For a scheduling product, the outcome might be: “A team lead can arrange a meeting without exchanging several messages.” For a conversation game, it might be: “A group can begin a shared conversation and move through turns comfortably.”

Ask people to attempt that task. A general question such as “What do you think?” can produce interesting suggestions, but it does not tell you whether the product delivers its main benefit.

Keep the task narrow enough to observe. If you try to evaluate onboarding, pricing, collaboration, analytics, and retention in one session, the feedback becomes harder to interpret.

## 2. Watch what happens before explaining the interface

Give participants the context they need, then let them try. Notice where they hesitate, make an unexpected choice, abandon a step, or ask for help.

[Nielsen Norman Group’s introduction to usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-101-introduction-to-usability/) recommends testing with representative users performing representative tasks and observing their behavior. Guiding someone through a difficult step can conceal a problem that another user will encounter alone.

If someone gets stuck, record the point of confusion before helping. Your ability to explain the product is different from the product’s ability to guide someone through it.

A friendly live audience is a useful starting point. Follow up with people who resemble your intended customers, using the product in circumstances closer to their normal work or life. Their needs may differ from those of fellow founders watching a demo.

## 3. Separate the observation, the problem, and the requested feature

Users often describe a solution before the underlying problem is clear. A request for notifications, for example, might reflect uncertainty about whether an action succeeded.

Use three fields in your feedback log:

| Field | What to capture |
| --- | --- |
| Observation | What the person did or said, and at which step. |
| Problem | What prevented or complicated the intended outcome. |
| Suggested solution | The participant’s idea, recorded without committing to build it. |

Keep interpretation separate from evidence. “They paused on the confirmation screen and asked whether the booking worked” is an observation. “The confirmation may be unclear” is an interpretation to investigate.

Before committing to a feature, ask: What were they trying to accomplish? What did they expect? What happened instead? How do they handle this today?

Those answers help you evaluate alternative fixes. The solution could be clearer language, a visible status, or a change to the flow rather than a new notification system.

## 4. Prioritize the barrier to the core outcome

Every suggestion does not belong in the next sprint. Compare feedback using a few practical questions:

- **Impact:** Does this prevent the main task, create a serious error, or introduce minor friction?
- **Repetition:** Have you observed it more than once? Record the count and context, rather than calling it “common” without evidence.
- **Audience fit:** Does it affect the users you intend to serve?
- **Confidence:** Did you observe it directly, or are you working from a suggestion or assumption?
- **Effort:** What is the smallest change that could address the problem?

Security, privacy, and data-loss issues can deserve immediate attention even if only one person reports them. For ordinary usability issues, these questions help you compare a blocker with a cosmetic preference.

With limited feedback, avoid treating a numerical score as certainty. Write down the reasoning behind your choice and what remains unknown.

Choose a manageable next change. Faster development, including development assisted by AI, still needs a clear decision about which problem is worth solving.

## 5. Give your developer a brief they can test

“Improve onboarding” leaves too many decisions unstated. A useful brief explains the user, the difficulty, the intended result, and how you will check the work.

Here is an illustrative example for a conversation game. **This is a hypothetical issue, not a finding reported from the Read Me playtest.**

| Brief field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| User and task | A first-time participant joining a group game. |
| Evidence to collect | Whether the participant can identify whose turn it is without help. |
| Problem hypothesis | The current-player state may be difficult to recognize. |
| Desired result | Participants can identify who should respond next. |
| Proposed first change | Add a visible current-player label and distinguish the active participant. |
| Acceptance criteria | The indicator matches the game state, updates when the turn advances, and remains readable on supported screen sizes. |
| User check | A new participant can identify the current player without a facilitator pointing it out. |

The brief gives the developer room to suggest a better implementation while keeping the outcome clear. A nontechnical founder can write the task, evidence, and desired result; a technical partner can help assess effort, dependencies, and implementation options.

## 6. Retest the change and keep a decision record

Shipping a fix answers “Did we build it?” Retesting answers “Did it help?”

Repeat the relevant task with suitable participants. Record whether they complete it, where they hesitate, and whether help is still needed. Check for new difficulties introduced by the change.

Keep a short record: the problem, the evidence, the decision, the change, and the result. Over time, that record helps your team avoid revisiting the same assumptions without new information.

A smoother task is evidence of improved usability. It does not by itself establish demand, willingness to pay, or product-market fit. Those questions need their own evidence, such as purchasing decisions, repeated use, and customer conversations.

## Common questions about MVP feedback

### Should we build every feature users request?

Use requests to investigate needs. Commit to a feature when you understand the problem it addresses, why it matters to your intended users, and why that solution deserves priority.

### What if we only have a handful of users?

Start with what you can observe, document the limits, and look for repeated difficulties. A small set of sessions can reveal useful problems without representing your whole market.

### How often should we collect feedback?

Build it into the development cycle. Collect feedback before committing to a significant change and again after users can try it. The right cadence depends on access to users and the scope of the work.
